# Swiggy Case Study — Orders Grew, But Revenue Didn’t

## Business Scenario

Over a three-month period, the company noticed something confusing:

- Orders increased by 12%
- Revenue increased by only 3%
- Customer complaints were rising
- Marketing spend increased by 20%

Leadership had a simple question:

"We’re getting more orders… so why isn’t revenue growing?"

This is the kind of open-ended business problem analysts often face. There was no clear starting point, just a gap between what the business expected and what actually happened.

My role was to investigate and understand the story behind these numbers.

---

## Step 1 — Clarifying the Objective

Before writing SQL or building dashboards, I first defined what we were really trying to solve.

The goal was to:
- Understand why revenue growth slowed
- Identify any customer or operational issues
- Provide practical recommendations

This step is important because analytics is not only about reporting metrics — it is about solving business problems.

---

## Step 2 — Identifying the Key Metric

If orders grow faster than revenue, one metric immediately becomes suspicious:

Average Order Value (AOV)

If orders increased by 12% but revenue only increased by 3%, it suggests customers are spending less per order.

This became the main hypothesis guiding the analysis.

---

## Step 3 — Validating the Numbers

After comparing the latest quarter with the previous one, the numbers confirmed the suspicion:

Average Order Value had dropped by 8%.

At this point, the real investigation began:  
Why were customers spending less per order?

---

## Step 4 — Impact of Discounts

The first area I explored was discounting.

The discount ratio had increased from 10% to 22%.

This showed that the company was heavily relying on discounts to drive order growth. While this helped increase order volume, it directly reduced revenue per order.

In simple terms, the business was buying growth through incentives.

---

## Step 5 — Free Delivery Campaigns

Next, I looked at delivery fees.

Marketing had launched free delivery campaigns during the same period.

This had two major effects:
- A direct loss of delivery fee revenue
- Customers started placing smaller orders

When delivery is free, customers no longer feel the need to combine items into one larger order. As a result, basket sizes shrink and AOV drops.

---

## Step 6 — Region Expansion Effect

A regional breakdown revealed another important pattern.

Newly expanded Tier-2 regions had significantly lower AOV compared to Tier-1 cities.

This meant the company had expanded into more price-sensitive markets. Orders increased because of new users, but revenue per order decreased.

This is a classic example of volume growth versus value growth.

---

## Step 7 — Customer Quality Analysis

Since marketing spend increased by 20%, I analyzed acquisition channels.

Paid advertising brought in a large number of new customers. However, these customers had noticeably lower average order values.

This suggested marketing campaigns were optimized for acquiring more users rather than higher-value users.

More customers does not always mean better customers.

---

## Step 8 — Delivery Performance and Complaints

Customer complaints were rising, so I examined delivery performance.

Average delivery time had increased from 28 minutes to 38 minutes.

In the food delivery industry, a 10-minute delay is significant. Slower deliveries lead to poor customer experience, fewer repeat orders, and weaker long-term revenue.

This explained the increase in complaints.

---

## Step 9 — Root Cause Summary

Putting all the findings together, the story became clear.

Growth was driven by:
- Heavy discounting
- Free delivery campaigns
- Expansion into lower-spending regions
- Acquisition of low-value customers
- Slower delivery times

The company successfully increased order volume, but at the cost of profitability and customer experience.

---

## Step 10 — Business Recommendations

Based on the analysis, I proposed four key actions.

1. Reduce blanket discounting and move toward targeted promotions for high-value customers.

2. Improve delivery operations and bring average delivery time back below 30 minutes.

3. Invest in retention through loyalty programs and personalized offers.

4. Measure customer acquisition cost versus lifetime value for each marketing channel and reduce spend on low-value acquisition sources.

---

## Final Takeaway

Orders grew, but revenue did not keep pace because the business optimized for order volume instead of customer value.

More customers does not automatically mean a healthier business.  
Sustainable growth comes from attracting and retaining the right customers.
